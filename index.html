c. 3000 BCE onward · Egypt, India

Leaf & Feather

The first fans were whatever was flat and close at hand: a palm leaf, a woven mat, a bird's wing. In Egypt they grew into the flabellum, a spray of ostrich feathers on a long, gold-covered pole carried beside the pharaoh. Tutankhamun was buried with eight fans, and "fan-bearer on the king's right hand" was a title of real rank.123

In India the word pankha comes from pankh, a feather or wing, and a small plumed pankhi already appears in the Ajanta paintings.45 Hand pankhas are woven from palm, bamboo, cane or grass, and fans of khus (vetiver root) were sprinkled with water so the breeze came back cool and fragrant. At court, a morchhal of peacock feathers was held behind the ruler as a sign that he protected his people.67

तह वाला पंखा

Japan, 9th century · China, Europe, 1000s–1800s

The Folding Fan

Round silk fans came from China; the folding fan was Japan's invention. The oldest one that survives is a hiōgi of thin cypress slats dated 877, found inside a statue at Tō-ji temple in Kyoto. A Japanese monk carried folding fans to the Song court as tribute in 988, and by the 1600s they had reached Europe.8910

In eighteenth-century Europe the fan became a fashion object of painted leaves and carved sticks. Late in the 1800s the Paris fanmaker Duvelleroy handed customers pamphlets on a secret "language of the fan". Curators now treat that code as a myth that helped sell fans.1112 The sandalwood skin on this stage nods to Mysore, a region known for carving its soft, fragrant sandalwood.13

छत का पंखा

1600s–early 1900s · India

The Pankha

A large hanging fan: a wooden frame with a cloth panel, often around four and a half metres wide, swung by a cord. A traveller saw one in Shah Jahan's court, and from the mid-1700s the British made the "punkah" standard in offices, churches and bungalows across India.1415

The pullers, pankha-wallahs, were usually from lower castes and often worked through the night. In private rooms the cord ran through a hole in the wall, so the puller worked outside, unseen. In 1898 they went on strike, and by the early twentieth century the electric ceiling fan had made the pankha obsolete.1416

What it asked of the hand: nothing. It asked someone else's.

बिजली का पंखा

1882 onward · United States, then everywhere

The Electric Fan

In 1882 Schuyler Skaats Wheeler fitted two blades to an electric motor and made the first electric fan. It had no cage.17 A few years later Philip Diehl mounted fan blades on the motor he had built for Singer sewing machines, hung it from the ceiling, and patented the electric ceiling fan in 1889.1819

Cages, oscillating heads and multi-speed regulators followed, in brass, black enamel and Bakelite. In Indian homes the stepped rotary regulator and its little red indicator lamp became part of every switchboard: five clicks between still air and a gale, with a slow spin-up after each one.

2009 · United Kingdom

Bladeless

Dyson launched the Air Multiplier in October 2009. A motor in the base pushes air out of a 1.3 mm slit running around a ring. The jet drags the surrounding room air along with it, which Dyson claims multiplies the flow about fifteen times.202122

The idea wasn't new; Toshiba had a similar concept in 1981.23 The blades didn't disappear either. They moved somewhere fingers can't reach, and the fan became a smooth object that gives no clue how it works.

What it asked of the hand: a button, or a remote from across the room.

2010s onward · India and worldwide

Brushless & smart

BLDC fans swap the old induction motor for permanent magnets and a small circuit board. A typical one draws about 28–35 W at full speed, against roughly 75 W for an older induction fan. In India that shift has been pushed by BEE star labels and by EESL, which sold 5-star BLDC fans under its UJALA programme.242526

With a chip on board, the fan got a remote, then an app, then a voice assistant. Speed, breeze patterns and sleep timers became software, and the regulator on the wall became optional.
